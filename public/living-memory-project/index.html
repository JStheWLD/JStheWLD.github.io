The Living Memory Project

The Living Memory Project exists to collect and preserve the cultural memory of American music.American music.

Think of it as a seed bank of who and what begat whom.

The stories behind American music are disappearing.

We find the people who were there and connect what they remember.

And we’re asking a few people to help.

The story · Studio B, 1960

During the session, part of the studio’s sound board breaks.
Grady Martin’s bass guitar comes out fuzzy, a buzzing, broken sound.
Engineer Glenn Snoddy is called in to fix it.
Producer Don Law keeps the sound.

1961.

“Don’t Worry” comes out with the fuzz left in.

1962.

Snoddy builds a circuit that makes the fuzz on purpose. Gibson sells it as the Maestro Fuzz-Tone.

1965 and on.

Keith Richards of the Rolling Stones plugs one in for “Satisfaction.” Then everyone does.

None of this isin the credits.

It’s never just one story.

Everyone who was there tells it their own way. Every story is part of the history.

These stories, this history,mostly live in people.

The Living Memory Projectgoes looking for themand their cultural memories.

Every story we find joins our Living Memory platform, a generative archive that finds and remembers how it all connects.

Why this matters.

1908

In 1908, a song hunter named John Lomax realized recording was changing music forever.

Before the 1920s,

songs traveled mouth to mouth.

Then radio and records arrived, and the whole country heard the same recordings.

He used the recording machine to save the songs it was erasing.

What we believe.

1 OF 5

We save seeds now.Like Lomax, we don’t wait to know why. A story is worth keeping the day it’s told.

2 OF 5

Story creates value.A song means more when you know where it came from. A guitar is worth more when you know who played it.

For example

David Gilmour’s “Black Strat,” the guitar he played on Pink Floyd’s The Dark Side of the Moon, sold at Christie’s for $3,975,000 in 2019. In March 2026 it sold again, for $14.55 million.

3 OF 5

Influence is music’s real family tree.Every musician learned from someone. Every sound came from somewhere, even a broken amplifier. We trace it all, every version.

4 OF 5

Every voice counts, even the tall tales.The player, the engineer and the fan up front each saw something different. Some stories agree, some don’t, and some get bigger every time they’re told. All of it is part of the story.

5 OF 5

The faster the world moves, the more people matter.Technology should bring us back to each other. As everything speeds up, people come first.

If you believe what we believe, join us in our mission to save our stories.
